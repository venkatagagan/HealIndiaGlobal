# Coronary angioplasty and stenting

This page gives a general explanation of planned coronary angioplasty with stenting. It can help you plan your trip and prepare questions for your cardiologist. Your own doctors will advise on your situation.

> **Important:** A heart attack needs emergency treatment where you are, not travel. Travelling for angioplasty is only for planned (non-emergency) treatment that your doctors agree can safely wait.

## What it is

Coronary angioplasty widens narrowed or blocked arteries that supply the heart muscle. It is also called percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or PTCA.

A cardiologist passes a thin, flexible tube (catheter) through an artery in your wrist or groin up to the heart. X-ray images and a special dye (contrast) show the arteries. A tiny balloon is inflated at the narrowed section to widen it. In most cases a stent is left in place to help keep the artery open. A stent is a small metal mesh tube. Most stents used today slowly release a medicine that helps stop the artery narrowing again. These are called drug-eluting stents.

The procedure is usually done under local anaesthetic, often with a mild sedative, so you are awake. It is often done in the same session as a coronary angiogram, straight after it.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Doctors may suggest angioplasty when angina continues despite medicines and tests show narrowing that a stent can treat. For some people, bypass surgery (CABG) or medicines alone may be the better option. This applies especially to people with narrowing in several arteries or other complex disease. Hospitals often decide together as a "heart team" of cardiologists and surgeons.

Only your doctors can decide whether angioplasty is right for you. Ask about the alternatives, and about the benefits and risks of each in your situation.

## Records to share for a quote

Upload:

- Your coronary angiogram report and, if possible, the video files. These are often on a CD, DVD or USB drive in DICOM format.
- Recent echocardiogram, ECG and stress test reports, for example a treadmill test, stress echo or nuclear scan
- A CT coronary angiogram report, if you have had one
- Details of any previous stents or bypass surgery, including your stent card if you have one
- Recent blood tests, especially kidney function, because the kidneys clear the contrast dye
- A full list of your medicines and doses, especially blood thinners
- Any allergies, particularly to contrast dye or iodine

## Tests usually done before treatment

Typical tests include:

- Blood tests: blood count, kidney function, blood sugar, clotting, and infection screening
- ECG and echocardiogram
- A coronary angiogram, if you do not have a recent one. This is often done just before the angioplasty, in the same session.
- During the procedure, the cardiologist may measure pressure inside the artery or take images from inside it. This helps decide whether a stent is needed and check its position.

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need. Some may be repeated in India.

## Typical time in hospital and in India

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital stay | About 1–3 days | Often one night; longer after complex procedures |
| Total time in India | About 5–10 days | Covers tests, the procedure and a check-up before you fly |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

After the procedure, nurses check the puncture site and your heart rhythm.

- **If the wrist was used:** a tight band is placed on it and loosened gradually.
- **If the groin was used:** you may need to lie flat for a few hours.

You will usually be encouraged to drink fluids to help clear the contrast dye.

For the first days, avoid heavy lifting and hard physical activity. Do not strain the arm or leg that was used. Your team will tell you exactly what to avoid and for how long.

You will be given medicines that stop blood clots forming in the stent. These are called antiplatelet medicines. You will usually take two of them, for a length of time set by your cardiologist.

> **Important:** Do not stop or interrupt your antiplatelet medicines unless your cardiologist tells you to. Stopping them early can allow a clot to form in the stent, which can cause a heart attack.

### After you return home

- Take enough medicine for the journey and for the time until you see a doctor at home. Ask for the generic (chemical) names so that a pharmacy at home can find the same medicines.
- Before you travel, arrange a follow-up appointment with a cardiologist at home. Take your procedure report and stent details with you.
- If a doctor or dentist plans a procedure, tell them you have a stent and take antiplatelet medicines. Do not stop the medicines without speaking to your cardiologist first.
- Ask about a cardiac rehabilitation programme near you.
- A stent treats one narrowed section. It does not cure coronary artery disease. Several things remain important:
  - not smoking
  - eating a heart-healthy diet
  - being active regularly
  - keeping blood pressure, cholesterol and diabetes under good control

## Fitness to fly

Your treating doctor will confirm when you are fit to fly. This is usually at a review before you leave hospital or shortly after. Things to discuss:

- **Blood clots (DVT):** ask whether you need compression stockings. On long flights, choose an aisle seat, drink water and move your legs regularly.
- **The puncture site:** it should be healing well, without swelling or bleeding, before you fly. Avoid lifting heavy bags.
- **Airline medical clearance:** after a recent heart procedure, some airlines ask for a medical form, often called a MEDIF. Check with your airline early.
- **Assistance:** ask for a wheelchair or airport assistance if walking long distances tires you.
- **Medicines and documents:** keep your antiplatelet medicines in your hand luggage, and plan your doses across time zones. Carry your stent card or procedure report.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Why do you recommend angioplasty for me rather than bypass surgery or medicines alone?
- How many stents might I need, and what type will you use?
- Will you go through my wrist or my groin?
- Which blood-thinning medicines will I take, and for how long?
- What should I do if I need surgery or dental treatment while taking them?
- Are my medicines available in my home country under other brand names?
- When can I safely fly home?
- What follow-up tests will I need at home?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** Get help immediately if you have any of these:
>
> - chest pain or pressure
> - sudden breathlessness
> - fainting
> - a very fast or irregular heartbeat
> - signs of a stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech)
> - bleeding from the puncture site that does not stop with firm pressure
> - a lump at the puncture site that grows quickly
> - a hand or foot that becomes cold, pale, numb or very painful
> - black stools, or vomiting blood
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number.
