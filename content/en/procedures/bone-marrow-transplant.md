# Bone marrow transplant (stem cell transplant)

This page explains what a bone marrow transplant involves and how much time to plan for in India, for you, your carer and any donor. It is general information to help you prepare questions for your transplant team.

## What it is

A bone marrow transplant (BMT), also called a haematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT), replaces damaged or diseased blood-forming cells with healthy stem cells.

First you receive **conditioning** treatment, which is high-dose chemotherapy and sometimes radiotherapy. The stem cells are then given through a drip, much like a blood transfusion; it is not an operation. Over the following weeks, the new cells settle in the bone marrow and start making blood cells. This is called engraftment.

There are two main types:

- **Autologous transplant** uses your own stem cells. They are collected in advance, usually from your blood after medicines that boost stem cell numbers, then stored and given back after the high-dose treatment.
- **Allogeneic transplant** uses stem cells from a donor. The donor may be a matched brother or sister, a matched unrelated donor found through a registry, or a half-matched (haploidentical) family member such as a parent, child or sibling. Donor cells are usually collected from the blood, or sometimes from the bone marrow under anaesthesia.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Transplant specialists may recommend a transplant for certain blood cancers, such as some leukaemias, lymphomas and myeloma. It may also be recommended for some non-cancer conditions, such as aplastic anaemia, thalassaemia major and some inherited immune or blood disorders.

The decision depends on the disease, how it has responded to treatment, your age and fitness, and whether a suitable donor is available. A transplant is an intensive treatment with serious risks. Ask about all your options, including other treatments and clinical trials. Only your transplant team can advise on what is right for you.

## Records to share for a quote

- Diagnosis reports: bone marrow biopsy and aspirate, flow cytometry, and cytogenetic and molecular test results
- Your full treatment history, including chemotherapy regimens with dates and responses, radiotherapy details and any previous transplant
- Recent blood counts, organ function tests and infection screening results
- Imaging such as PET-CT or CT, as DICOM files where relevant
- HLA (tissue type) results for you and for any family members already tested
- Your transfusion history, including any reactions or antibodies
- Your current medicines, allergies and other health conditions, and a referral letter from your haematologist

## Tests usually done before treatment

- Tests to confirm the current state of the disease, such as a bone marrow test and scans
- HLA tissue typing for you and possible family donors. Brothers, sisters, parents and children may be tested early. Ask the transplant centre whether testing can be done in your home country, and which laboratories' results they accept.
- Organ function tests: heart (ECG and echocardiogram), lungs (lung function tests and a chest scan), and kidney and liver function
- Infection screening, and a dental check so that any infection can be treated beforehand
- Insertion of a central line for treatment and blood tests
- A discussion about fertility and, where possible, fertility preservation, because conditioning treatment often affects fertility
- For an allogeneic transplant, the donor's own health checks, infection screening and consent

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need; some may be repeated in India. Your donor will also need a visa and time in India; ask your coordinator early.

## Typical time in hospital and in India

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Assessment and preparation | Often 1–3 weeks or more | Tests, donor work-up, line insertion and, for autologous transplant, stem cell collection |
| Hospital stay | About 3–6 weeks | From conditioning until blood counts recover; often longer for allogeneic transplant |
| Staying near the centre | Often several weeks after discharge | Allogeneic patients are often asked to stay close to the centre for around the first 100 days |
| Total time in India | About 2–4 months or more | Autologous is usually shorter; allogeneic is usually longer |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

- **In hospital:** you will usually stay in a protected room with strict hygiene rules and limited visitors.
- **Side effects:** conditioning can cause mouth sores, nausea, diarrhoea, tiredness and hair loss. You may need blood and platelet transfusions until your new cells start working.
- **After discharge:** expect frequent clinic visits, especially at first, for blood tests, transfusions and medicines. These include medicines to prevent infection and, after an allogeneic transplant, medicines that calm the immune system.
- **Graft-versus-host disease (GvHD):** after an allogeneic transplant, the donor's immune cells can attack your body. This can cause a rash, diarrhoea or liver problems. Your team will monitor you closely and explain what to watch for.
- **Accommodation:** choose clean accommodation close to the centre, ideally with a kitchen so food can be prepared safely. Your team will give you rules about food, cleaning, plants, pets and visitors.
- **Your carer:** transplant centres usually require a dedicated adult carer to stay with you throughout. Ask what your centre requires. Your carer needs their own visa, and should be healthy and up to date with the vaccinations the team recommends.

### After you return home

- The immune system takes many months, sometimes longer, to recover. Continue infection precautions as advised.
- You will probably need to repeat your childhood vaccinations on a schedule set by your team. Avoid live vaccines until your team says they are safe.
- Arrange a local haematologist **before** you leave India for blood tests, medicine levels and monitoring for GvHD. Bring a full written transplant summary.
- Protect your skin from the sun, and attend long-term check-ups for later effects on hormones, bones, the heart and fertility.
- Recovery can be emotionally demanding for you and your family. Ask about support services.

## Fitness to fly

- **Your team decides:** your transplant team will base the decision on your blood counts, any infections, GvHD and your medicines.
- **Infection risk:** wear a mask, use hand gel and avoid crowded areas in airports and on the plane.
- **Medicines:** carry more than enough medicine in your hand luggage, with a letter listing it. Some medicines must never be missed, so plan doses across time zones.
- **Central line:** keep the line covered and bring dressing supplies.
- **Blood counts and oxygen:** low platelets or anaemia may need checking before you fly. If you need oxygen or other medical help on board, the airline may require a medical clearance form, often called a MEDIF.
- **Blood clots:** long flights carry a risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis, DVT), so ask about precautions.

Your treating doctor confirms when you are fit to fly, usually at a review before discharge or shortly after.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Why is a transplant recommended for me now, and what are the alternatives?
- Will it be autologous or allogeneic? If I need a donor, who should be tested, and how?
- What conditioning treatment will I have, and what are its effects?
- How long will I be in hospital, and how long must I stay near the centre afterwards?
- What are the main risks, such as infection, GvHD, organ problems, graft failure or relapse, and how are they managed?
- What do you require from my carer?
- What will my donor go through, and how long must they stay in India?
- How will this affect my fertility, and can it be preserved?
- What follow-up will I need at home, and how will you share information with my haematologist?
- Which vaccines will I need afterwards, and when?
- How do I reach the transplant team at any hour after discharge?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** After a transplant, infections and other complications can become serious very quickly. Contact your transplant team or get emergency help immediately if you have:
>
> - a fever, shivering or chills, or you suddenly feel unwell
> - shortness of breath, a new cough or chest pain
> - bleeding, unusual bruising, or tiny red or purple spots on the skin
> - severe diarrhoea or vomiting, or you cannot eat, drink or take your medicines
> - a new rash, yellowing of the skin or eyes, or sudden weight gain or swelling
> - redness, swelling, pain or leaking around your central line
> - confusion, a severe headache or a seizure
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number, and tell staff that you have had a stem cell transplant.
