# Cataract surgery

This page explains cataract surgery, the lens choices you may be offered, and how to plan your time in India. It is general information to help you talk with your eye surgeon.

## What it is

A cataract is a clouding of the eye's natural lens. It can make your vision blurred, dim or glary. Cataract surgery removes the cloudy lens and replaces it with a clear artificial lens called an intraocular lens (IOL).

The most common technique is phacoemulsification ("phaco"). It uses ultrasound to break up the lens and remove it through a very small cut. Some clinics also use a laser for certain steps. The operation is usually done under local anaesthesia, with numbing drops or an injection around the eye, sometimes with light sedation. You go home the same day. Each eye is operated on separately.

There are several types of replacement lens:

- **Monofocal lenses** focus at one distance. Many people still need glasses for reading or other tasks.
- **Toric lenses** also correct astigmatism.
- **Multifocal or extended-depth-of-focus lenses** aim to reduce dependence on glasses at several distances. Some people notice glare or halos with them.

Ask which lenses suit your eyes and lifestyle. They differ in price and in possible side effects.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Eye doctors may suggest surgery when a cataract affects daily life, for example reading, driving or recognising faces. They may also suggest it when the cataract makes other eye conditions harder to examine or treat. In the early stages, new glasses and brighter lighting may help for a while.

Only your eye doctor can decide if and when surgery is right for you. Other eye conditions, such as glaucoma, diabetic eye disease or macular degeneration, can affect how much your vision improves, so make sure your surgeon knows about them.

## Records to share for a quote

- Your most recent eye examination report, with the diagnosis and vision measurements
- Your current glasses prescription
- Records of any previous eye surgery, especially laser vision correction (LASIK or PRK), because this affects lens calculations
- Details of other eye conditions and treatments, such as glaucoma drops or injections for the retina
- Your general health conditions, such as diabetes and high blood pressure, your current medicines and any allergies
- Tell the team if you take, or have taken, alpha-blocker medicines for prostate or urinary problems (for example tamsulosin), because they can affect the surgery

## Tests usually done before treatment

- Vision testing and measurement of your prescription
- Biometry, which measures the length and curvature of the eye to calculate the power of the replacement lens
- A corneal assessment, and in some cases a count of the cells lining the back of the cornea
- Eye pressure measurement
- An examination of the retina with dilated pupils, and sometimes an OCT scan of the retina
- Blood sugar and blood pressure checks, plus other tests depending on your general health

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need; some may be repeated in India. If you wear contact lenses, you may be asked to stop wearing them for a while before the measurements.

## Typical time in hospital and in India

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Assessment | Usually 1–2 days | Measurements to choose the lens power |
| Hospital stay | None (day surgery) | You go home the same day |
| Total time in India, one eye | About 3–7 days | Assessment, surgery and a check soon after |
| Total time in India, both eyes | About 1–2 weeks | Eyes are usually done on separate days; your doctor decides the gap |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

- Mild discomfort, watering, a gritty feeling and blurred vision are common at first.
- You will usually use eye drops for some weeks to prevent infection and reduce inflammation. Use them exactly as prescribed. Ask the nurse to show you, or your companion, how to put them in.
- Wear the eye shield at night, and whenever you are told to. Do not rub or press on the eye.
- Keep soap and water out of the eye. Avoid swimming, dusty places and eye make-up for as long as advised.
- Follow your surgeon's advice about bending and heavy lifting.
- Do not drive until your doctor confirms your vision meets driving standards.

### After you return home

- Vision often settles over a few weeks. You may need new glasses once the eye has healed, and your doctor will tell you when to get them.
- Finish the full course of drops, and arrange a follow-up check with a local eye doctor.
- Months or years later, some people develop clouding of the thin membrane behind the new lens. This is sometimes called "secondary cataract" and is usually treated with a quick laser procedure.
- Keep the record of your lens type and power, because future eye doctors will need it.

## Fitness to fly

- **Gas in the eye:** routine cataract surgery does not usually involve placing gas in the eye. If a gas or air bubble is placed inside your eye, which is more common in retina surgery, you must not fly until your eye doctor confirms it is safe. The bubble expands at altitude and can seriously damage the eye.
- **Dry cabin air:** use lubricating drops if your doctor recommends them, and keep all your drops in your hand luggage.
- **Blood clots:** long flights carry a general risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis, DVT). Walk and stretch regularly.
- **Assistance:** if your other eye also sees poorly, ask the airline for airport assistance. Medical clearance from the airline (often called a MEDIF) is not usually needed after routine surgery, but ask your doctor if there were complications.

Your treating doctor confirms when you are fit to fly, usually at a review before discharge or shortly after.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Which lens options suit my eyes and lifestyle, and what are the trade-offs?
- How might my other eye conditions affect the result?
- Will you operate on one eye or both, and how long will you leave between them?
- What type of anaesthesia will you use?
- What are the risks, such as infection, raised eye pressure, swelling at the back of the eye or retinal detachment, and how are they managed?
- How long will I need eye drops, and what is each one for?
- When can I fly, drive, swim and bend down?
- Will I get a written record of my lens type and power?
- When should I get new glasses?
- Who do I contact if I have a problem after I go home?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** Contact your eye surgeon or get urgent eye care straight away if you have:
>
> - eye pain that is getting worse
> - a sudden drop in vision
> - increasing redness or discharge
> - new flashes of light, a shower of floaters, or a shadow or curtain across your vision
> - eye pain with nausea or vomiting
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number or go to an urgent eye service.
