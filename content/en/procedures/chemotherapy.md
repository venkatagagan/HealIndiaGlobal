# Chemotherapy

This page explains how chemotherapy is usually planned and given, and what to think about if you are having some or all of it in India. Facing cancer treatment away from home is a lot to take on. The practical points below are meant to make planning a little easier. They are general information only.

## What it is

Chemotherapy uses medicines to destroy cancer cells or stop them from growing. It can be given in several ways:

- through a drip into a vein (intravenously), sometimes through a long-term line called a port or a PICC line
- as tablets or capsules
- as injections

It is usually given in **cycles**: a treatment period followed by a rest period that lets your body recover. A cycle often repeats every 1–4 weeks, and a full course can last weeks to months. Chemotherapy is often combined with other treatments, such as surgery, radiotherapy, targeted therapy or immunotherapy.

Most chemotherapy is given as day care, so you go back to your accommodation the same day. Some treatment plans, or side effects, need a hospital stay.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Cancer doctors (oncologists) may recommend chemotherapy:

- to try to cure the cancer
- to shrink a tumour before surgery or radiotherapy
- to lower the chance of the cancer coming back after surgery
- to control the cancer and ease symptoms

The plan depends on the type and stage of the cancer, test results, your general health and your own wishes. Many hospitals discuss each case at a multidisciplinary tumour board, a meeting of different cancer specialists. Ask whether yours will be discussed there.

Only your oncologists can advise on what is right for you. It is reasonable to ask about all your options, including other treatments, clinical trials and supportive (palliative) care. Seeking a second opinion is also reasonable.

## Records to share for a quote

- **Pathology reports:** biopsy and pathology reports, including special test results (for example receptor status, immunohistochemistry, or genetic and molecular tests)
- **Slides and blocks:** ask the hospital whether they need your original biopsy slides and tissue blocks for review. If they do, ask your pathology laboratory how to release them, and ask how to carry them safely.
- **Scans:** imaging reports and the images themselves (CT, MRI or PET-CT), as DICOM files on a disc, USB drive or download link
- **Previous treatment:** summaries of surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, including drug names, doses, dates, number of cycles and side effects
- **Blood tests:** recent results, such as blood counts, kidney and liver function, and tumour markers if relevant
- **Health and medicines:** your current medicines, allergies and other health conditions, plus a referral letter from your oncologist if you have one

## Tests usually done before treatment

- A review of your pathology, sometimes with repeat testing on the original tissue
- Staging scans if recent ones are not available, such as CT, MRI or PET-CT
- Blood tests, such as blood counts, kidney and liver function, and screening for hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV before some treatments
- Heart tests (an ECG or echocardiogram) for medicines that can affect the heart
- Height and weight measurements, which are used to calculate doses
- Insertion of a port or PICC line, if needed

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need; some may be repeated in India. If you may want children in the future, ask about fertility preservation, such as egg or sperm freezing, **before** treatment starts.

## Typical time in hospital and in India

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital stay | Usually none (day care) | Some treatment plans or side effects need one or more nights in hospital |
| First cycle | About 1 week or more in India | Includes assessment, tests and planning before the first treatment |
| Full course | Usually weeks to months | Depends on your plan; ask about shared care with a doctor at home |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

> **Important:** Some patients have part of their treatment in India and then continue at home with a local oncologist, which is called shared care. Whether this is safe and practical depends on your treatment plan and what is available where you live. Ask early, and make sure your home team receives the full written plan, with drug names, doses and dates.

## Recovery

### In India

- **Side effects:** these depend on the medicines. They can include tiredness, nausea, hair loss, mouth sores, changes in taste, tingling in the hands or feet, diarrhoea or constipation, and low blood counts. Many can be prevented or eased, so tell your team early.
- **Infection risk:** chemotherapy can lower your white blood cells. Wash your hands often and avoid crowds and people who are unwell. Eat freshly cooked, hot food and drink sealed bottled or properly filtered water. Avoid street food, raw salads and undercooked food. Ask whether you need a special diet while your counts are low.
- **Temperature:** keep a thermometer with you and use it if you feel unwell.
- **Line care:** if you have a port or PICC line, keep the area clean and dry, and learn when it needs flushing.
- **Looking after yourself:** rest, take gentle walks, eat what you can and keep drinking fluids. Counselling and support from your companion matter too, so ask what support the hospital offers.

### After you return home

- Bring a written treatment summary that lists the regimen, doses, dates, blood results, side effects and next steps.
- Arrange a local oncologist **before** you travel, for blood tests between cycles and for any problems.
- Keep to the flushing schedule for any port or line.
- Ask before having any vaccine. Live vaccines are usually avoided during chemotherapy.
- Use effective contraception during treatment, and for as long afterwards as your team advises.
- You will need follow-up check-ups and scans. Ask what longer-term effects to watch for.

## Fitness to fly

- **Blood counts:** ask when your counts are likely to be at their lowest, and plan travel around that time. Wear a mask and use hand gel in airports and on the plane.
- **Blood clots:** cancer and some treatments increase the risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis, DVT). Ask about prevention on long flights.
- **Oxygen and assistance:** low platelets or anaemia may need checking before you fly. If you need oxygen or other medical help on board, the airline may require a medical clearance form, often called a MEDIF. Ask for wheelchair assistance if you are tired.
- **Medicines:** carry all your medicines in your hand luggage with a letter listing them. Some medicines are controlled in certain countries, so check the import rules for your home country.
- **Insurance:** travel insurance often excludes cancer treatment and pre-existing conditions, so check your policy carefully.

Your treating doctor confirms when you are fit to fly, usually at a review before discharge or shortly after.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- What is the aim of this treatment for me?
- Which medicines will I have, how many cycles, how often, and for how long overall?
- Which parts must happen in India, and could any be given at home?
- What side effects should I expect, and how will they be prevented or managed?
- Will I need a port or PICC line, and how do I look after it when I travel?
- What are my other options, including clinical trials?
- How might treatment affect my fertility, and can it be preserved first?
- What should I eat and avoid while my blood counts are low?
- Which symptoms mean I must call you or go to an emergency department, and who answers at any hour?
- When will I be fit to fly, and what precautions should I take?
- What information will you send to my doctors at home?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** During and after chemotherapy, infections can become serious very quickly. Get urgent help, day or night, if you have:
>
> - a high temperature (your team will tell you the exact level to act on), shivering or chills, or you suddenly feel unwell, even without a fever
> - shortness of breath or chest pain
> - unexplained bleeding or bruising, or blood in your urine or stools
> - severe vomiting or diarrhoea, or you cannot keep fluids down
> - redness, swelling, pain or leaking around your line or port
> - pain, swelling or redness in one leg or arm
> - confusion or unusual drowsiness
>
> Tell staff that you are having chemotherapy. In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number or your cancer team's helpline.
