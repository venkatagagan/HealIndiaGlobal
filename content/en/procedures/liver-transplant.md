# Liver transplant (living donor)

This page gives a general explanation of living-donor liver transplantation. It covers both the person who receives the liver (the recipient) and the donor. It can help you plan and prepare questions for the transplant team. Your own doctors will advise on your situation.

> **Important:** Transplants in India are regulated under the Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act and its rules. A hospital authorisation committee must approve a living donation before surgery. You and your donor will need documents that prove your relationship. For foreign nationals, this usually includes certification from your country's embassy or high commission. Buying or selling organs is illegal.

## What it is

A liver transplant replaces a diseased liver with a healthy one. In a living-donor liver transplant, surgeons remove part of a healthy donor's liver and transplant it into the recipient. The liver can regrow. Over the following weeks and months, both the donor's remaining liver and the transplanted part grow towards a normal working size.

Both operations are major surgery under general anaesthesia and can take many hours.

This page covers living-donor transplants, the usual route for patients who travel for a liver transplant. Livers from deceased donors are shared out through national and state systems with their own rules. Ask the hospital whether this applies to you.

After the transplant, the recipient takes medicines to prevent rejection (immunosuppressants) for life.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Doctors may suggest a liver transplant for:

- advanced liver disease (cirrhosis), which has various causes
- some liver cancers
- some inherited or metabolic conditions, including in children

Sudden (acute) liver failure is a medical emergency. It needs care where you are, not travel.

Not everyone is suitable. The team checks your heart, lungs and kidneys, any infections, your nutrition and your other conditions. The donor is checked just as carefully. Only the transplant team can decide whether a transplant is right for you and whether your donor is suitable. Ask about the alternatives for your condition.

## Records to share for a quote

**Recipient:**

- A summary from your liver specialist (hepatologist)
- Recent blood tests, such as liver function, clotting (INR), kidney function and blood count
- Severity scores such as MELD (or PELD for children), if calculated
- CT or MRI scans of the liver, as images (DICOM files) and reports
- Endoscopy and biopsy reports and, for liver cancer, staging scans and tumour marker results
- Discharge summaries for complications such as bleeding, fluid build-up in the abdomen (ascites) or confusion (encephalopathy)
- A full list of your medicines and doses, any allergies and your vaccination records
- If relevant, information about past alcohol use. Some transplant teams ask for evidence that you have stopped drinking.

**Donor:**

- Medical history, recent blood tests, blood group, height and weight, and a list of medicines and any allergies

**Relationship documents:** the hospital's transplant coordinator will give you the current list of documents needed for approval. Start collecting them early, because approvals take time. Your donor will also need a visa. Ask your coordinator which visa category applies.

## Tests usually done before treatment

**Recipient:** typical tests include:

- blood group, detailed blood tests and infection screening
- a heart check-up, such as an ECG, an echocardiogram and sometimes stress tests
- lung function tests
- a CT or MRI scan of the liver and its blood vessels
- an endoscopy
- cancer screening suited to your age and sex
- dental, nutrition, psychological and social assessments

**Donor:** typical tests include:

- blood group compatibility, liver blood tests and infection screening
- CT or MRI scans to measure the size of the liver and map its blood vessels and bile ducts
- sometimes a liver biopsy, for example to check for fatty liver
- heart and lung checks
- a psychological assessment with counselling

The team will make sure your donor understands the risks and is donating freely.

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need. Some may be repeated in India.

## Typical time in hospital and in India

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Recipient: hospital stay | About 2–4 weeks | Includes time in intensive care after surgery |
| Donor: hospital stay | About 1–2 weeks | Depends on recovery from surgery |
| Total time in India (recipient) | About 2–3 months or more | Covers checks, legal approvals, surgery and close follow-up; the donor's total time is usually shorter, and the team will advise |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

After intensive care, the recipient moves to a ward and starts eating and walking. Frequent blood tests and scans check the new liver and help the team adjust medicine doses. After you leave hospital, you stay near it for close follow-up.

Your team will teach you about your medicines and how to prevent infection:

- clean your hands often
- avoid crowds and people who are unwell
- wear a mask if advised
- follow food-safety advice, including avoiding raw or undercooked food and street food

The donor will be encouraged to walk early. The donor is followed up until the team is happy with healing and liver tests.

### After you return home

- **Arrange a local liver or transplant team before you travel.** At home you will need regular blood tests, checks of your medicine levels and specialist reviews.
- Take your immunosuppressants exactly on time, every day, for life. Never stop or change them without your transplant team.
- Do not switch to a different brand of immunosuppressant without advice. Your blood levels may change.
- Avoid alcohol unless your team says otherwise. Check with your team before starting any new medicine, herbal remedy or supplement.
- Ask before having any vaccine. Some live vaccines are not suitable after a transplant.
- Protect your skin from the sun and have regular skin checks.
- Donors should avoid heavy lifting for as long as advised. They should also keep their follow-up appointments for liver tests and wound checks.

## Fitness to fly

Your treating doctors will confirm when the recipient and the donor are each fit to fly. This is usually at a review before leaving hospital or shortly after. Things to discuss:

- **Infection risk:** airports and planes are crowded. Wear a mask if advised and clean your hands often.
- **Medicine timing:** keep all medicines in your hand luggage, with extra supplies in case of delays. Ask the team how to time your doses across time zones.
- **Wounds and lifting:** both of you will have a large wound in the abdomen. Do not lift luggage, and ask for help at check-in and on board.
- **Blood clots (DVT):** ask whether you need compression stockings. Choose an aisle seat, drink water as advised and move regularly.
- **Airline medical clearance:** after major surgery, some airlines ask for a medical form, often called a MEDIF. Check with your airline early.
- **Assistance and documents:** ask for airport assistance. Carry your prescriptions, a doctor's letter and your discharge summary.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Am I suitable for a liver transplant, and is my donor suitable?
- How much of the donor's liver will be removed, and what are the risks for the donor?
- Which documents and approvals do we need? How long does approval usually take at this hospital?
- What are the main risks for me, and how long will I be in hospital?
- Which immunosuppressants will I take, and are they available at home?
- How often will I need blood tests at home, and who will do them?
- What are the signs of rejection or infection?
- When can each of us fly home?
- What follow-up does my donor need?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** **Recipient:** get help immediately if you have any of these:
>
> - a fever or chills
> - yellowing of the skin or eyes
> - dark urine or pale stools
> - pain or swelling in the abdomen
> - confusion or unusual sleepiness
> - vomiting blood, or black stools
> - breathlessness or chest pain
> - a wound that is red or leaking
> - vomiting or diarrhoea that stops you keeping your medicines down
>
> **Donor:** get help immediately if you have any of these:
>
> - a fever
> - severe pain in the abdomen
> - yellowing of the skin or eyes
> - a wound that is red or leaking
> - a painful, swollen leg
> - chest pain or breathlessness
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number, then tell your transplant team.
