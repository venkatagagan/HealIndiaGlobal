# Total hip replacement

This page gives a general explanation of total hip replacement. It can help you plan your trip and prepare questions for your orthopaedic surgeon. Your own doctors will advise on your situation.

## What it is

The hip is a ball-and-socket joint. Total hip replacement is also called total hip arthroplasty. The surgeon replaces two damaged parts with artificial ones: the ball at the top of the thigh bone, and the socket in the pelvis. The new parts may be made of metal, ceramic and medical-grade plastic. They may be fixed with bone cement, or designed so that the bone grows onto them (uncemented).

Surgeons can reach the hip from the back, the side or the front. Each approach has its own recovery advice. The operation is usually done under spinal anaesthesia or general anaesthesia. For some patients, other operations such as hip resurfacing may be options.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Doctors may suggest hip replacement when the hip is very painful and stiff and this limits walking, daily activities or sleep. Possible causes include:

- arthritis
- damage to the bone from poor blood supply (avascular necrosis)
- an earlier injury
- a hip problem from childhood

Surgery is usually considered when non-surgical treatments have not helped enough.

A broken hip (hip fracture) is usually treated urgently where it happens. It is not suitable for planned medical travel.

Only your doctors can decide whether surgery is right for you. Ask about the alternatives, including continuing non-surgical care.

- Any allergies, including to metals or antibiotics
- Your height and weight, and a short description of your pain and how far you can walk

## Tests usually done before treatment

Typical tests include:

- Blood tests: blood count, kidney and liver function, blood sugar and HbA1c, clotting, blood group, and infection screening
- A urine test
- An ECG and a chest X-ray, and for some patients an echocardiogram
- Updated X-rays of the hip and pelvis, and sometimes a CT scan for planning
- Checks for any infection that should be treated before surgery, for example of the skin, urine or teeth
- An assessment by the anaesthetist

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need. Some may be repeated in India.

## Recovery

### In India

You will usually be helped to stand and walk with a frame or crutches on the day of surgery or the next day. Physiotherapists will teach you exercises. They will also show you how to do everyday movements safely:

- getting in and out of bed
- sitting down and standing up
- using the toilet
- climbing stairs

Your team may ask you to follow **hip precautions** for a while. These lower the risk of the new joint coming out of place (dislocating). Whether you need them depends on the approach and the implant. Common examples are:

- not bending the hip too far
- not crossing your legs
- not twisting on the operated leg

Your team will tell you whether these apply to you and for how long.

You may be given blood-thinning injections or tablets, compression stockings and leg exercises. These lower the risk of blood clots. Before you fly, the wound will be checked and any stitches or clips removed if needed.

### After you return home

- Before you travel, arrange a physiotherapist near your home. Take your exercise programme with you.
- Prepare your home. A raised toilet seat, a firm chair of good height and clear walkways can help. Avoid low sofas and seats if your team advises this.
- Use your walking aid until you are told you can stop. Strength and walking usually keep improving over months.
- Follow your team's advice on driving, sexual activity, and returning to work and sport.
- Keep your implant details.
- Tell doctors and dentists you have a joint replacement. Ask your surgeon whether you will need antibiotics before future procedures.
- Arrange a follow-up review, and have X-rays done if they are requested.

## Fitness to fly

Your treating doctor will confirm when you are fit to fly. This is usually at a review before you leave hospital or shortly after. Things to discuss:

- **Blood clots (DVT):** the risk is higher after hip surgery, and long flights add to it. Ask about blood thinners, compression stockings and exercises. Get up and walk when it is safe to do so.
- **Seating and hip precautions:** aircraft and car seats can be low. Ask whether you need a cushion, an aisle seat or a seat with extra legroom. Also ask how to get in and out of cars safely.
- **Assistance:** ask for a wheelchair and help at airports. Take your walking aid with you.
- **Airport security:** metal implants can set off security detectors. Carry your implant card or a letter from your surgeon.
- **Airline medical clearance:** after recent surgery, some airlines ask for a medical form, often called a MEDIF. Check with your airline early.
- **Medicines:** keep them in your hand luggage, with your prescription.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Which approach will you use, and what does that mean for my recovery?
- Which implant materials will you use, and why?
- Will I need to follow hip precautions? For how long?
- What are the main risks, such as infection, dislocation, blood clots or a difference in leg length?
- How will my pain be controlled?
- How will blood clots be prevented, both during my stay and on the flight?
- How long will I need walking aids?
- When can I safely fly home, and how should I sit during the flight?
- What equipment will I need at home?
- What should my physiotherapist at home know?
- Will I get an implant card?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** Get help immediately if you have any of these:
>
> - chest pain or sudden breathlessness
> - a painful, swollen calf or thigh
> - sudden severe hip pain
> - a leg that looks shorter or turned
> - being unable to move the leg or put weight on it
> - a fever or chills
> - a wound that is red, hot or leaking
> - heavy bleeding
> - a foot that becomes cold, pale or numb
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number.
