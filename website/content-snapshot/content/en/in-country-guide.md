# In-country guide

This guide covers your time in India: what to do in an emergency, how hospitals work, how to communicate, how to eat and drink safely, how to get around and what to expect culturally. Each city guide has local details.

## Emergency numbers and help

| Who | Number or where to find it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| National emergency (police, fire, ambulance) | **112** | India's national emergency number |
| Ambulance | **108** | Operates in many states; confirm the local ambulance number when you arrive |
| Your hospital | Emergency department and international patient desk | Save both numbers on your first day |
| Heal India Global | 24/7 helpline and SOS button in the app (Support screen) | Help with logistics, translators and transport; not an emergency service |
| Your embassy or consulate | Your government's official website | Lost passports, serious incidents, legal problems |

> **Emergency:** If someone is seriously ill or injured, call 112, or 108 for an ambulance where available. Then call the hospital and press SOS in the app. Give the operator your exact location: the name of your hotel or hospital, the area and a landmark, or the location pin from your phone's map. Keep your accommodation address written down in English and in the local language.

The SOS button alerts your coordinator and our support team, and shares your location if you allow it. It **does not** call an ambulance or the police.

> **Check before you travel:** Emergency and ambulance services differ from state to state. When you arrive, ask your hospital or accommodation for the local ambulance number and the nearest emergency department.

## Your support team

- **Your coordinator (Heal India Global)** helps with logistics, bookings, translators, transport, accommodation and messages with the hospital.
- **The hospital's international patient desk** handles appointments, billing questions, interpreters and letters for visa matters.
- **The 24/7 helpline** in the app is for urgent help with logistics at any hour.

Save these contacts on your first day, and agree with your companion how you will stay in touch.

## At the hospital

### Practical points

- Bring your passport, visa, booking confirmation and previous reports to your first appointment and to admission. The hospital may keep copies.
- Expect to register, have a photo taken for your records and wear an identification band while admitted.
- Hospitals usually issue an attendant pass to one companion. Visiting hours are limited, especially in intensive care. Follow the rules on how many visitors can come at once.
- Ask the billing desk to explain anything not covered by your booking **before** you agree to it. Keep every receipt.
- Keep copies of every report, scan and bill, and upload them to the app.
- Ask early about your discharge summary, so it is ready when you leave.

### Hospital etiquette

- Address doctors as "Doctor" or "Dr" followed by their surname. Senior nurses are often called "Sister".
- Doctors usually see patients on ward rounds at set times. Stay in your room around those times, with your questions written down.
- Keep your voice and phone volume low. Do not photograph other patients or staff without permission.
- Smoking is not allowed in hospitals or in other public places.
- Tipping doctors and nurses is not expected. To thank your care team, use the hospital's feedback form or leave a review.
- Help prevent infection: clean your hands often, limit the number of visitors, and check before bringing flowers or outside food into the ward.
- You can ask for a doctor or chaperone of a particular gender for examinations.
- Tell the international patient desk about your religious, cultural or dietary needs, such as prayer times or halal, vegetarian or Jain meals.

### Consent

Before a procedure you will be asked to sign consent forms. You are entitled to understand what you sign. Ask questions, take time to think and ask for a qualified interpreter if you need one. Do not sign anything you do not understand.

## Communication and translators

- Doctors in larger hospitals usually speak English. Nurses and support staff may speak mainly the local language. The city guides list local languages.
- Request a translator in the app (Support, then Get Translator), or ask your coordinator or the international patient desk. Book interpreters early for important appointments: consultations, consent discussions, the day of your procedure and discharge.
- Speak slowly and use short sentences. Repeat key instructions back to check you have understood them.
- Ask for written instructions, and write down the names of your medicines.
- Translation apps help with everyday conversations, but they are not reliable enough for medical decisions.

## Food and water

- Drink sealed bottled water, or water that your hospital or hotel confirms has been filtered. Check that seals are unbroken. Avoid tap water and ice of unknown origin. If you have a sensitive stomach, brush your teeth with bottled water too.
- Eat freshly cooked food that is served hot. Peel fruit yourself. While you are recovering, avoid raw salads, cut fruit sold on the street and street food in general.
- Follow the diet your care team gives you, for example after weight-loss surgery or if you have diabetes or kidney disease. If your immunity is low, for example after a transplant or during chemotherapy, follow the hospital's food-safety rules strictly. Ask before eating anything brought in from outside.
- Indian food can be spicy. Ask for "less spicy" or "no chilli".
- Vegetarian food is widely available. Halal and Jain meals can usually be arranged. Many dishes contain dairy (ghee, paneer, curd), so ask if you avoid it. Beef is not served in many places.
- Clean your hands before eating.

> **Important:** If you have diarrhoea with a fever or blood, cannot keep fluids down, are recovering from surgery or have low immunity, contact your hospital promptly. Dehydration can be serious and can affect how your medicines work.

## Getting around

- **Hospital or package transport.** Use it after surgery and for appointments, where it is included.
- **App-based cabs** are available in large cities. Check that the car number and driver match the app, sit in the back and share your trip with your companion.
- **Prepaid taxis** are available at official counters at airports and many railway stations.
- **Auto-rickshaws** are three-wheelers that are open to the air and can be bumpy. They are not ideal after surgery. Agree the fare before you start, or use the meter or a ride app.
- **Metro rail** in many cities is air-conditioned but crowded at peak times. Stations have security checks, and some trains have coaches reserved for women.
- **Driving.** Traffic drives on the left, and road conditions vary. We do not recommend driving yourself while you are a patient.
- **Walking.** Pavements can be uneven or blocked, and traffic does not always stop for pedestrians. Cross carefully, ideally with your companion.
- **Seatbelts.** Wear a seatbelt in every car, including in the back seat.
- **Timing.** Allow extra time for traffic, especially at peak hours and during the monsoon.

## Staying safe

### Personal safety

- Keep your passport in the hotel safe and carry a copy or a photo of it. Keep valuables out of sight.
- Use ATMs inside banks, hospitals or shopping centres. Watch out for pickpockets in crowds.
- Avoid isolated areas at night, and share your location with your companion.
- Beware of touts at airports and stations who say your hotel is closed or offer "official" tours. Use only the transport and accommodation arranged in the app.
- Be wary of calls or messages that claim to come from your hospital or from us and ask for payment or personal details. Check in the app or with your coordinator first. We will never ask for your card details or one-time passwords.
- Women travellers can reduce unwanted attention by dressing modestly. Use app cabs with trip sharing, and use the women-only metro coaches where they exist.
- Check your government's travel advice for India, and register with your embassy if your country offers this.

### Health and environment

- **Heat.** Drink plenty of water, avoid the midday sun and wear light clothing. Dizziness, headache, nausea or confusion in hot weather need prompt attention.
- **Mosquitoes.** Use repellent and wear long sleeves, especially around dawn and dusk and during and after the monsoon. Mosquitoes spread infections such as dengue. Tell your doctor promptly if you get a fever.
- **Air quality.** Pollution can be high in some cities, especially in north India in late autumn and winter. Check the daily air quality index (AQI), stay indoors on bad days and ask your doctor about masks if you have a heart or lung condition.
- **Animals.** Keep away from stray dogs and monkeys. If you are bitten or scratched, wash the wound well and get medical care the same day, because rabies is a risk in India.
- **Floodwater.** Avoid walking through floodwater during the monsoon, especially if you have a wound.
- **Medicines.** Buy medicines only from licensed pharmacies. Check with your treating doctor before taking anything new, including herbal and Ayurvedic products.

## Cultural norms

- **Greetings.** "Namaste", said with palms pressed together, is polite everywhere. Handshakes are common in hospitals and business settings. Some people prefer not to shake hands with someone of the opposite gender, so let them offer first.
- **Right hand.** Use your right hand to eat, and to give or receive things.
- **Places of worship.** Remove your shoes at temples, mosques, gurdwaras and in many homes. Cover your shoulders and knees. At gurdwaras, and at some temples and mosques, cover your head too.
- **Photography.** Ask before photographing people. Photography is not allowed in some religious sites, at airport security or near military areas.
- **Public behaviour.** Keep public displays of affection discreet.
- **Body language.** A side-to-side tilt of the head often means "yes", "I understand" or "all right".
- **Conversation.** Questions about your family, marriage or work are common friendly conversation. You do not have to answer.
- **Bargaining** is normal in markets, but not in shops with fixed prices, shopping centres or pharmacies.
- **Festivals** such as Diwali, Holi, Eid, Durga Puja and Christmas bring crowds and heavy traffic, and around Diwali there are fireworks and often poorer air quality. Hospitals stay open, but some outpatient clinics close. Your coordinator plans appointments around holidays.
- **Alcohol** rules vary by state, and some days are "dry days" when alcohol is not sold. Avoid alcohol during treatment and recovery unless your doctor says otherwise.
- **Timing.** Schedules can change at short notice. Confirm appointments the day before.
- **Tipping** in restaurants and for drivers or porters is appreciated but not required. Check whether a service charge is already on the bill.

## Practical information

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Time zone | India Standard Time (UTC+5:30), the same across the country, with no daylight saving |
| Currency | Indian rupee (INR, ₹) |
| Electricity | 230 volts, 50 Hz; plug types C, D and M |
| Driving side | Left |

- Power cuts can happen. Hospitals have backup power, but keep your phone charged and carry a power bank.
- Mobile data coverage is generally good in cities. Hospitals and hotels usually offer Wi-Fi for messages and video calls.
- Hotels and service apartments usually offer laundry.
