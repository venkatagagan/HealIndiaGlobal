# ACL reconstruction

This page gives a general explanation of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. It can help you plan your trip and prepare questions for your orthopaedic surgeon. Your own doctors will advise on your situation.

## What it is

The ACL is one of the main ligaments inside the knee. It connects the thigh bone to the shin bone. It keeps the knee stable when you twist, pivot or change direction. A torn ACL does not usually heal back together on its own.

In ACL reconstruction, the surgeon replaces the torn ligament with a graft. The graft is usually tissue taken from your own body (an autograft). It commonly comes from one of these:

- the hamstring tendons
- the patellar tendon
- the quadriceps tendon

Less often, donor tissue (an allograft) is used, depending on availability.

The operation is done by keyhole (arthroscopic) surgery, under spinal or general anaesthesia. Damage to the meniscus (the knee's shock absorbers) or to the cartilage may be treated in the same operation.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Doctors may suggest reconstruction in these situations:

- your knee gives way in daily life or sport
- you want to return to sports that involve pivoting or sudden changes of direction
- other structures in the knee are also injured

Some people manage well with a structured physiotherapy programme alone, without surgery.

Surgeons often prefer to wait until the knee swelling settles and movement returns before operating. They may recommend physiotherapy beforehand, which is sometimes called "prehabilitation".

Only your doctors can decide whether surgery is right for you. Ask about the non-surgical option and how it compares for your goals.

## Tests usually done before treatment

Typical tests include:

- An examination of the knee by the surgeon, including stability tests
- An MRI scan, if yours is old or not clear enough
- X-rays of the knee
- Blood tests
- An ECG, depending on your age and health
- An assessment by the anaesthetist

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need. Some may be repeated in India.

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital stay | Day surgery to 2 nights | Some patients go home the same day |
| Total time in India | About 1–2 weeks | Covers early physiotherapy and a wound check before you fly |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

You will usually walk with crutches at first. Depending on your surgeon's approach, you may be given a knee brace. Early physiotherapy has three aims:

- controlling swelling
- getting the knee fully straight
- switching on the thigh muscles again

Ice, raising the leg and pain relief help in the first days. Your team will tell you how to care for the small wounds. They may also advise steps to lower the risk of blood clots.

### After you return home

Rehabilitation is a central part of ACL reconstruction and continues for many months.

- Before you travel, arrange a physiotherapist who has experience of ACL rehabilitation. Take your surgeon's rehabilitation protocol with you.
- You will progress in stages. First you walk without crutches, then return to driving and work, then running, then sport. Each step depends on how your knee is doing, not just on time.
- Returning to pivoting sports usually takes many months. Your surgeon decides when it is safe, often based on strength and stability tests.
- Book follow-up reviews with your surgeon in India by video consultation, or with a specialist at home.

## Fitness to fly

Your treating doctor will confirm when you are fit to fly. This is usually at a review before you leave hospital or shortly after. Things to discuss:

- **Blood clots (DVT):** leg surgery and moving less increase the risk, and long flights add to it. Ask about blood thinners, compression stockings and exercises.
- **Brace and crutches:** tell the airline you are travelling with them. An aisle seat or a seat with extra legroom makes it easier to keep the leg comfortable and to move.
- **Swelling:** keep moving your ankle. Raise the leg when you are allowed to.
- **Assistance:** ask for help at airports.
- **Airline medical clearance:** after recent surgery, some airlines ask for a medical form, often called a MEDIF. Check with your airline early.
- **Documents:** carry your operation notes, rehabilitation protocol and medicines in your hand luggage.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Do I need surgery, or could I try a rehabilitation programme first?
- Which graft do you recommend for me, and why?
- Are there other injuries you will treat, such as a meniscus tear?
- Will I need a brace? How long will I use crutches?
- Which rehabilitation protocol do you follow, and can I have it in writing?
- When can I safely fly home?
- When might I return to work, driving, running and sport?
- What are the risks, including the new ligament tearing again?
- What should I look for in a physiotherapist at home?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** Get help immediately if you have any of these:
>
> - chest pain or sudden breathlessness
> - a painful, swollen calf
> - a fever or chills
> - a wound that is red, hot, leaking or opening
> - severe pain that keeps getting worse
> - a foot that becomes cold, pale or numb
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number.
