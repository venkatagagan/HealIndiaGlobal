# Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG)

This page gives a general explanation of coronary artery bypass grafting, which is often called CABG, bypass surgery or heart bypass. It can help you plan your trip and prepare questions for your heart team. Your own doctors will advise on your situation.

## What it is

The coronary arteries carry blood to your heart muscle. Fatty deposits can narrow or block them, which is called coronary artery disease. The heart muscle may then not get enough blood and oxygen. This can cause chest pain (angina) or breathlessness, or lead to a heart attack.

In CABG, a heart surgeon takes healthy blood vessels from elsewhere in your body, usually from inside the chest wall, the leg or the forearm. The surgeon attaches them so that blood can flow around ("bypass") the narrowed sections. Many people need more than one graft in the same operation.

CABG is major heart surgery done under general anaesthesia. Most often the surgeon opens the breastbone (sternum). Some operations use a heart-lung machine that does the work of the heart and lungs for a time ("on-pump"). Others are done while the heart keeps beating ("off-pump"). Some patients may be able to have an approach with smaller cuts (minimally invasive surgery). Your surgeon will explain which approach they suggest and why.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Doctors may suggest CABG in several situations:

- several coronary arteries are narrowed
- the main left coronary artery is affected
- angina continues despite medicines
- angioplasty (stenting) is not suitable

The decision depends on detailed tests, your other health conditions and your own preferences.

Only your doctors can decide whether CABG is right for you. Ask about the alternatives, including medicines alone and angioplasty. Ask about the benefits and risks of each option in your situation. A second opinion before major heart surgery is reasonable.

- A recent echocardiogram (heart ultrasound) report
- Recent ECGs and any stress test results
- Discharge summaries from earlier hospital stays, for example after a heart attack or stent procedure
- Recent blood test results, such as blood count, kidney function, blood sugar and HbA1c
- A full list of your medicines and doses, especially blood thinners, and any allergies
- Details of other conditions, such as diabetes, kidney disease, lung disease or a previous stroke

## Tests usually done before treatment

Typical tests include:

- Blood tests: blood count, kidney and liver function, blood sugar, clotting, blood group and cross-match, and infection screening
- ECG and echocardiogram
- A coronary angiogram, if you do not have a recent one
- Chest X-ray
- An ultrasound (Doppler) scan of the neck arteries, often for older patients or people who have had a stroke
- Lung function tests for some patients
- An assessment by the anaesthetist

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need. Some may be repeated in India.

## Recovery

### In India

After surgery you will be closely monitored in intensive care, then you will move to a ward. Nurses and physiotherapists will help you:

- sit up
- breathe deeply
- cough while supporting your chest with a pillow
- start walking within the first few days

Tiredness and soreness in the chest wall are common. So is some swelling where a vessel was taken from your leg or arm.

Before you leave hospital, the team checks your wounds, heart rhythm and blood tests. They also teach you how to care for your cuts. They may start early cardiac rehabilitation, which combines supervised exercise with education.

### After you return home

Your breastbone takes weeks to months to heal. Until your doctor says it is safe, you will be advised not to lift heavy objects, push or pull hard, or drive. Energy usually returns gradually.

- Ask your doctor to refer you to a cardiac rehabilitation programme near your home.
- Take your medicines exactly as prescribed. Do not stop blood thinners or other heart medicines unless a doctor tells you to.
- Arrange follow-up with a cardiologist at home before you travel. Take your full discharge summary and operation notes with you.
- Lifestyle also matters for long-term heart health. This means not smoking, eating a heart-healthy diet, being active regularly, and keeping blood pressure, cholesterol and diabetes under good control.
- Low mood and poor sleep can happen after heart surgery. Tell your doctor if they do not get better.

## Fitness to fly

Your treating doctor will confirm when you are fit to fly. This is usually at a review before you leave hospital or shortly after. Things to discuss:

- **Blood clots (DVT):** long flights increase the risk of clots in the legs. Ask whether you need compression stockings or medicine to prevent clots. Choose an aisle seat, drink water, and walk or move your legs regularly.
- **Your healing breastbone:** lifting bags into overhead lockers strains the chest. Ask for help with luggage.
- **Breathing and oxygen:** tell the airline well in advance if you get breathless easily or might need oxygen.
- **Airline medical clearance:** after recent surgery, some airlines ask for a medical form completed by your doctor, often called a MEDIF. Check with your airline early.
- **Assistance:** ask for a wheelchair or airport assistance when you book.
- **Medicines and documents:** keep medicines in your hand luggage, with a copy of your prescription and discharge summary.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Why do you recommend CABG for me rather than angioplasty or medicines alone?
- How many grafts do you expect, and which blood vessels will you use?
- Will the operation be on-pump, off-pump or minimally invasive, and why?
- What are the main risks for someone with my health conditions?
- How long will I spend in intensive care and in hospital?
- Which medicines will I need afterwards, and for how long?
- When can I safely fly home, and what will I need for the journey?
- What rehabilitation and follow-up should I arrange at home?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** Get help immediately if you have any of these:
>
> - chest pain like your old angina, or any severe chest pain
> - sudden breathlessness
> - fainting
> - a very fast or irregular heartbeat
> - signs of a stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech)
> - coughing up blood
> - a painful, swollen leg
> - a wound that is red, hot, leaking or opening, especially with a fever
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number.
