# Heart valve replacement and repair

This page gives a general explanation of heart valve repair and replacement. It can help you plan your trip and prepare questions for your heart team. Your own doctors will advise on your situation.

## What it is

Your heart has four valves: the aortic, mitral, tricuspid and pulmonary valves. They keep blood flowing in the right direction. A valve can become narrowed so that it does not open fully (stenosis). It can also become leaky, so that blood flows backwards (regurgitation). Either problem can strain the heart.

The treatment options are:

- **Valve repair:** the surgeon fixes your own valve. This is done most often for the mitral valve.
- **Valve replacement:** your valve is replaced with a new one. There are two main types:
  - **Mechanical valves** are made from long-lasting man-made materials. You need blood-thinning medicine (an anticoagulant) for the rest of your life.
  - **Tissue (biological) valves** are made from animal tissue. They usually do not need lifelong anticoagulation, but they can wear out over time. You may need further treatment in future.

The valve can be reached in different ways:

- **Open-heart surgery:** usually through the breastbone, with a heart-lung machine, under general anaesthesia.
- **Minimally invasive surgery:** through smaller cuts, for some patients.
- **Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI, also called TAVR):** a new valve is guided along a thin tube (catheter), usually through an artery in the groin. It is placed inside the narrowed aortic valve without opening the chest.

Catheter-based options also exist for some other valve problems.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Doctors may suggest valve treatment when a valve problem is severe. They may also suggest it when the problem causes symptoms such as breathlessness, chest pain, fainting or tiredness, or starts to affect how well the heart pumps.

The choice between repair, replacement, open surgery and TAVI depends on which valve is affected, the type of problem, your age, your other health conditions and your preferences.

Only your doctors can decide what is right for you. Ask them to explain the pros and cons of each option for you, including mechanical versus tissue valves. If you might want to become pregnant in future, tell your doctors early. This affects the choice of valve and medicines.

- Discharge summaries and recent blood test results
- A full list of your medicines and doses, especially blood thinners, and any allergies

## Tests usually done before treatment

Typical tests include:

- Blood tests: blood count, kidney and liver function, blood sugar, clotting, blood group, and infection screening
- ECG, chest X-ray and echocardiogram
- Transoesophageal echo for some patients. A probe is passed down the food pipe under sedation to get clearer pictures.
- A coronary angiogram or CT coronary angiogram to check the heart arteries
- A CT scan of the heart and blood vessels, especially if TAVI is being considered
- A dental check. Infections in the mouth should be treated before a new valve is fitted.
- Lung function tests for some patients
- An assessment by the anaesthetist

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need. Some may be repeated in India.

| Total time in India: open surgery | About 3–4 weeks | Covers tests, surgery, recovery and checks before you fly |
| Hospital stay: TAVI | About 2–5 days | Recovery is usually quicker than after open surgery |
| Total time in India: TAVI | About 1–2 weeks | Covers tests, the procedure and a check-up before you fly |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

Recovery from open surgery is similar to other major heart operations. You spend time in intensive care, then on the ward. You will do breathing exercises and walk with a physiotherapist. Recovery after TAVI is usually quicker, and the main wound is a small puncture in the groin.

If you have a mechanical valve, you will start an anticoagulant such as warfarin. You will need regular blood tests, called INR tests, to keep the dose in the right range. The team will tell you your target range. They will also explain how diet, alcohol and other medicines can affect it.

### After you return home

- **Anticoagulation:** if you need lifelong anticoagulation, arrange INR monitoring at home **before** you travel. Choose a clinic or doctor who has your target range and current dose in writing.
- **New medicines:** many medicines interact with anticoagulants. Check with a doctor or pharmacist before you start any new medicine, herbal remedy or supplement.
- **Preventing infection:** keep your teeth and gums healthy and have regular dental check-ups. Tell every dentist and doctor that you have a replaced or repaired valve. Ask your cardiologist whether you need antibiotics before certain dental treatments.
- **Valve card:** keep a record of your valve's type, size and manufacturer. You will need it for future scans and procedures.
- **Breastbone healing:** after open surgery, follow the limits your surgeon gives on lifting and driving. Join a cardiac rehabilitation programme if one is available.
- **Valve sounds:** mechanical valves often make a soft clicking sound. Ask your team what is normal.

## Fitness to fly

Your treating doctor will confirm when you are fit to fly. This is usually at a review before you leave hospital or shortly after. Things to discuss:

- **Blood clots (DVT):** ask whether you need compression stockings. Choose an aisle seat, drink water and move your legs regularly.
- **Anticoagulant timing:** if you take warfarin, ask when your next INR test is due and how to time your doses across time zones.
- **Wounds and lifting:** ask for help with luggage, especially after open surgery.
- **Airline medical clearance:** after heart surgery, some airlines ask for a medical form, often called a MEDIF. Check with your airline early.
- **Assistance:** ask for a wheelchair or airport assistance when you book.
- **Documents:** carry your medicines in your hand luggage. Take your prescription, discharge summary and valve card too.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Can my valve be repaired, or does it need replacing?
- Would you recommend a mechanical or a tissue valve for me, and why?
- Am I suitable for TAVI or minimally invasive surgery?
- Will I need blood thinners? Which ones, and for how long?
- What is my INR target range, and how do I arrange tests at home?
- What are the main risks for someone like me?
- Will I need antibiotics before dental treatment in future?
- Which valve will you use, and will I get a valve card?
- When can I safely fly home?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** Get help immediately if you have any of these:
>
> - chest pain
> - sudden breathlessness
> - fainting
> - a very fast or irregular heartbeat
> - signs of a stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech)
> - a fever with chills
> - a wound that is red, hot or leaking
> - a painful, swollen leg
> - unusual bleeding while on blood thinners, such as blood in your urine, black stools, vomiting blood or a severe headache
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number.
