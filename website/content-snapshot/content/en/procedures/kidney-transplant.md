# Kidney transplant (living donor)

This page gives a general explanation of living-donor kidney transplantation. It covers both the person who receives the kidney (the recipient) and the donor. It can help you plan and prepare questions for the transplant team. Your own doctors will advise on your situation.

> **Important:** Transplants in India are regulated under the Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act and its rules. A hospital authorisation committee must approve a living donation before surgery. You and your donor will need documents that prove your relationship. For foreign nationals, this usually includes certification from your country's embassy or high commission. Buying or selling organs is illegal.

## What it is

A kidney transplant places a healthy kidney from a donor into a person whose own kidneys have failed. The new kidney is usually placed in the lower abdomen. The recipient's own kidneys are usually left in place. The donor's kidney is often removed by keyhole (laparoscopic) or robot-assisted surgery. Both operations are done under general anaesthesia.

This page covers living-donor transplants, where a relative donates a kidney. Kidneys from deceased donors are shared out through national and state systems with their own rules. Ask the hospital whether this applies to you.

After a transplant, the recipient takes medicines to prevent rejection (immunosuppressants) for life.

## Why doctors may recommend it

For people with kidney failure, a transplant may be an option instead of long-term dialysis. Not everyone is suitable. The transplant team checks your heart health, any infections, any history of cancer and your other conditions. Donors must be healthy, fully informed and donating of their own free will. They are checked just as carefully.

Only the transplant team can decide whether a transplant is right for you and whether your donor is suitable. Ask about the alternatives, including continuing haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis.

## Records to share for a quote

**Recipient:**

- A summary from your kidney specialist (nephrologist) and your kidney function results over time
- Dialysis details: the type, the schedule and your access (fistula, graft or catheter)
- Heart tests, such as an ECG, echocardiogram, stress test or angiogram, if you have had them
- Infection screening results, your blood group and any tissue-typing or crossmatch results
- Any previous transplants, blood transfusions or pregnancies, because these can affect antibody levels
- A full list of your medicines and doses, any allergies and your vaccination records

**Donor:**

- Medical history, recent blood and urine tests, blood group and any scans
- A list of medicines and any allergies

**Relationship documents:** the hospital's transplant coordinator will give you the current list of documents needed for approval. Start collecting them early, because approvals take time. Your donor will also need a visa. Ask your coordinator which visa category applies.

## Tests usually done before treatment

**Recipient:** typical tests include:

- blood group, tissue typing (HLA) and crossmatch with the donor, and antibody tests
- blood tests, including kidney and liver function, blood count and clotting
- infection screening, for example for HIV, hepatitis B and C, and tuberculosis
- a heart check-up and a chest X-ray
- scans of the abdomen and blood vessels
- cancer screening suited to your age and sex
- dental, psychological and social assessments

**Donor:** typical tests include:

- blood group, tissue typing and crossmatch
- detailed kidney function and urine tests
- blood pressure and blood sugar checks
- infection screening
- a CT scan of the kidneys and their blood vessels
- heart and lung checks
- a psychological assessment with counselling

The team will make sure your donor understands the risks and is donating freely.

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need. Some may be repeated in India.

## Typical time in hospital and in India

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Recipient: hospital stay | About 7–14 days after surgery | Close monitoring of the new kidney and medicine levels |
| Donor: hospital stay | About 3–7 days | Depends on recovery from surgery |
| Total time in India | About 4–8 weeks or more | Covers checks on donor and recipient, legal approvals, surgery and close early follow-up |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

In the first weeks, the recipient has frequent blood tests and clinic visits. These check how the kidney is working and help the team adjust medicine doses. Sometimes the new kidney takes time to start working fully. Dialysis may then be needed for a short period.

Your team will teach you about your medicines and how to prevent infection:

- wash your hands often
- avoid crowds and people who are unwell
- wear a mask if advised
- follow food-safety advice, including avoiding raw or undercooked food and street food

The donor will be encouraged to walk early. The donor will have check-ups before being cleared to travel.

### After you return home

- **Arrange a local transplant team before you travel.** At home you will need regular blood tests, checks of your medicine levels and specialist reviews.
- Take your immunosuppressants exactly on time, every day, for life. Never stop or change them without your transplant team.
- Do not switch to a different brand of immunosuppressant without your transplant team's advice. Your blood levels may change.
- Check with your transplant team before starting any new medicine, herbal remedy or supplement.
- Ask before having any vaccine. Some live vaccines are not suitable after a transplant.
- Protect your skin from the sun and have regular skin checks. Immunosuppressants increase the risk of skin cancer.
- Donors need long-term follow-up too, including checks of blood pressure, kidney function and urine. Arrange this at home.

## Fitness to fly

Your treating doctors will confirm when the recipient and the donor are each fit to fly. This is usually at a review before leaving hospital or shortly after. Things to discuss:

- **Infection risk:** airports and planes are crowded. Wear a mask if advised and clean your hands often.
- **Medicine timing:** keep all medicines in your hand luggage, with extra supplies in case of delays. Ask the team how to time your doses across time zones.
- **Blood clots (DVT):** ask whether you need compression stockings. Choose an aisle seat, drink water as advised and move regularly.
- **Lifting:** neither the recipient nor the donor should lift heavy luggage.
- **Airline medical clearance:** after major surgery, some airlines ask for a medical form, often called a MEDIF. Check with your airline early.
- **Assistance and documents:** ask for airport assistance. Carry your prescriptions, a doctor's letter and your discharge summary.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Am I suitable for a transplant, and is my donor suitable?
- Which documents and approvals do we need? How long does the approval process usually take at this hospital?
- What are the main risks for me and for my donor?
- Which immunosuppressants will I take? What are their side effects, and are they available in my home country?
- How often will I need blood tests at home, and who will do them?
- What are the signs of rejection or infection?
- Might I need dialysis after the transplant? For how long?
- When can each of us fly home?
- What long-term follow-up does my donor need?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** **Recipient:** get help immediately if you have any of these:
>
> - a fever or chills
> - passing much less urine than usual
> - pain or swelling over the new kidney
> - sudden weight gain or swelling
> - breathlessness or chest pain
> - vomiting or diarrhoea that stops you keeping your medicines down
>
> **Donor:** get help immediately if you have any of these:
>
> - a fever
> - severe pain in the abdomen
> - a wound that is red or leaking
> - a painful, swollen leg
> - chest pain or breathlessness
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number, then tell your transplant team.
