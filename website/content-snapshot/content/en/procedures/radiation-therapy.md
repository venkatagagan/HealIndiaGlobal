# Radiation therapy (radiotherapy)

This page explains how radiotherapy is usually planned and given, and how to plan your time in India around it. It is general information to help you prepare for conversations with your cancer team.

## What it is

Radiotherapy uses carefully targeted, high-energy radiation to destroy cancer cells while limiting the dose to healthy tissue nearby. There are several main types:

- **External beam radiotherapy:** a machine outside the body, usually a linear accelerator, aims radiation at the treatment area. Techniques such as IMRT (intensity-modulated radiotherapy) and IGRT (image-guided radiotherapy) shape and guide the dose. Each session is painless. The radiation itself takes only minutes, though positioning takes longer.
- **Stereotactic radiotherapy (SRS or SBRT):** very precise, higher doses given in a small number of sessions.
- **Brachytherapy:** a radioactive source is placed inside or next to the tumour, either temporarily or as permanent "seeds".

Some centres also offer other forms, such as proton therapy, for selected cancers.

A course can be anything from a single session to about 7 weeks. Treatment is usually given once a day on weekdays, with a break at weekends.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Radiation oncologists may recommend radiotherapy:

- to try to cure a cancer, on its own or alongside surgery or chemotherapy
- to lower the chance of a cancer coming back after surgery
- to ease symptoms such as pain

It is sometimes used for non-cancerous conditions too. The plan depends on the type and stage of the cancer, any previous treatment and your general health.

Only your doctors can advise on what is right for you. Ask about all your options, and whether radiotherapy will be combined with other treatments.

## Records to share for a quote

- **Pathology:** biopsy and pathology reports. Ask whether the hospital needs your original slides or tissue blocks.
- **Scans:** imaging reports and the images themselves (CT, MRI or PET-CT) as DICOM files on a disc, USB drive or download link
- **Previous treatment:** surgery notes and chemotherapy summaries
- **Previous radiotherapy:** full details of any radiotherapy you have had, including dates, the area treated, the dose and, if possible, the plan files. This is essential, because healthy tissues can only safely receive a limited total amount of radiation.
- **Implanted devices:** tell the team about any pacemaker, defibrillator or other implanted device, because radiotherapy can affect them
- **Health and medicines:** your current medicines, allergies and recent blood tests

## Tests usually done before treatment

- A consultation with a radiation oncologist and a review of your records, sometimes with new scans
- A planning (simulation) CT scan in the exact position you will be treated in, sometimes combined with an MRI or PET scan
- A custom immobilisation device, such as a mask for head, neck or brain treatment, or a body mould
- Tiny permanent skin marks in some centres, to help line up each treatment
- Blood tests, and a dental check before head and neck radiotherapy
- A discussion about fertility if the treatment area includes the pelvis

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need; some may be repeated in India. After the planning scan, the team needs some days to design and check your plan before treatment starts.

## Typical time in hospital and in India

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Planning | About 1–2 weeks | Planning scan, mask or mould, and time to design your plan |
| Hospital stay | Usually none (outpatient) | Brachytherapy or some combined treatments may need admission |
| Treatment course | From 1 session to about 7 weeks | Usually daily on weekdays; stereotactic courses are short |
| Total time in India | About 2–8 weeks | Depends on planning, course length and checks |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

- **Daily routine:** sessions are short. You lie still while the machine moves around you, and staff watch and talk to you through a camera and intercom.
- **Side effects:** these usually build up gradually during the course and depend on the area treated. Tiredness is common. The skin in the treated area may become red, dry, itchy, darker or may peel. Head and neck treatment can cause a sore mouth and throat, and pelvic treatment can affect the bowel and bladder. Hair loss only happens in the area being treated.
- **Skin care:** wash gently, use only the products your team recommends, wear loose clothing and keep the area out of the sun.
- **Eating and drinking:** eat well and drink plenty of fluids. If eating is difficult, ask to see a dietitian.
- **Reviews:** you will usually see your doctor or nurse regularly during the course. Raise any problems early.
- **Safety around others:** external beam radiotherapy does not make you radioactive, so it is safe to be with other people, including children. If you have brachytherapy, ask whether any precautions are needed and for how long.

### After you return home

- Some side effects may continue, or even get worse, for a short time after the course ends before they settle.
- Some effects can appear months or years later. Ask what to watch for.
- Keep your radiotherapy summary, including the doses, and give a copy to your doctor at home.
- Follow-up visits and scans will be needed, so arrange them with your local team before you leave India.
- The treated skin may stay more sensitive to the sun, so protect it.

## Fitness to fly

- **Tiredness:** tiredness can be strong towards the end of a course. Ask for wheelchair or airport assistance and plan rest stops.
- **Skin and medicines:** wear loose clothing. Carry creams and medicines in your hand luggage, with a letter for any liquids over the airport limit.
- **Blood clots:** cancer increases the risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis, DVT). Ask about precautions on long flights.
- **Permanent seed implants:** if you had permanent radioactive seed implants, carry the card or letter you are given. Radiation detectors at airports and borders may pick them up.
- **Paperwork:** if you need oxygen or other medical help on board, the airline may require a medical clearance form, often called a MEDIF.

Your treating doctor confirms when you are fit to fly, usually at a review before discharge or shortly after.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- What is the aim of radiotherapy for me?
- Which type and technique will you use, and why?
- How many sessions will I need, and how long will planning take before I start?
- What side effects should I expect during and after the course, and how are they managed?
- Are there long-term effects I should know about?
- Will I have chemotherapy or other treatment at the same time?
- How should I care for my skin, mouth or bowels during treatment?
- What happens if a session is missed, for example if I am unwell?
- Do I need to take any precautions around other people?
- When can I fly home, and what follow-up will I need?
- Will you send a full treatment summary, including doses, to my doctor at home?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** Get urgent help if you have:
>
> - a fever or chills, especially if you are also having chemotherapy
> - severe pain, bleeding, or skin that breaks down, weeps heavily or looks infected
> - difficulty swallowing fluids, or you cannot eat or drink
> - severe diarrhoea or vomiting, or signs of dehydration
> - shortness of breath, chest pain, or pain and swelling in one leg
> - new back pain with weakness or numbness in the legs, or new problems controlling your bladder or bowels
> - new weakness, confusion, a severe headache or a seizure
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number.
