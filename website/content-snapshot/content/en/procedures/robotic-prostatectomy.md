# Robotic prostatectomy (prostate cancer surgery)

This page explains robot-assisted radical prostatectomy, how to plan your stay in India, and what recovery usually involves. It is general information to help you prepare for conversations with your urologist.

## What it is

A radical prostatectomy is an operation to remove the whole prostate gland and the seminal vesicles. It is usually done to treat prostate cancer that has not spread beyond the prostate. Nearby lymph nodes are sometimes removed at the same time.

In a robot-assisted ("robotic") operation, the surgeon sits at a console and controls robotic arms that hold the camera and instruments. These pass through several small cuts in the abdomen, so this is a form of keyhole surgery. The surgeon performs the operation; the robot only moves the instruments under the surgeon's control. The operation is done under general anaesthesia.

After the prostate is removed, the bladder is joined back to the urethra (the tube that carries urine out of the body). A urinary catheter is left in place while this join heals.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Urologists may suggest surgery for prostate cancer that appears to be confined to the prostate. They consider the grade and stage of the cancer, your age, your general health and your preferences.

Surgery is one of several options. Others include:

- **active surveillance**, which means careful monitoring, for some lower-risk cancers
- **radiotherapy** (external beam or brachytherapy), sometimes with hormone therapy
- **focal treatments**, in selected cases
- **watchful waiting**

Each option has different side effects. It can help to discuss your options with both a urologist and a radiation oncologist. Only your doctors can advise on what is right for you.

## Records to share for a quote

- PSA test results over time, with dates
- Your biopsy pathology report, including the Gleason score or Grade Group and how many samples contained cancer. Ask whether the hospital needs the original slides or blocks for review.
- Your prostate MRI report and images, as DICOM files on a disc, USB drive or download link
- Any staging scans you have had, such as a PSMA PET scan, bone scan or CT
- Your other medical conditions, any previous abdominal or pelvic surgery, your current medicines (especially blood thinners) and any allergies
- A short description of your current urinary and erectile function. Your doctor may also ask you to fill in a questionnaire.

## Tests usually done before treatment

- A PSA test and a review of your biopsy pathology, sometimes by the hospital's own pathologists
- A prostate MRI if a recent one is not available
- Staging scans when needed, such as a PSMA PET-CT or a bone scan and CT
- Blood tests (blood count, kidney function, clotting and blood sugar), a urine test, an ECG and a chest X-ray
- An anaesthesia assessment, sometimes with heart tests

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need; some may be repeated in India. You may also be taught pelvic floor muscle exercises, which many teams recommend starting before surgery.

## Typical time in hospital and in India

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital stay | About 1–3 days | You walk early and go home with the catheter in place |
| Urinary catheter | Usually about 1–2 weeks | Removed at a clinic visit, sometimes after a bladder X-ray |
| Total time in India | About 2–3 weeks | Includes catheter removal and, often, discussing the final pathology report |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

- **Moving and clots:** walk regularly. Use blood-thinning injections or compression stockings if advised.
- **Catheter care:** keep it clean, and use the leg bag by day and the larger bag at night. Bladder spasms and some blood in the urine can happen, so ask what is normal for you.
- **Bowels:** avoid straining. Prevent constipation with fluids, fibre and any stool softeners prescribed.
- **Lifting and driving:** do not lift anything heavy or drive until your surgeon says you can.
- **Catheter removal:** leakage after the catheter comes out is common. Have pads ready.
- **Pathology results:** your surgeon will discuss the final pathology report with you. It may change your follow-up plan.

### After you return home

- **Continence:** bladder control often improves over weeks to months, helped by regular pelvic floor exercises. How quickly it recovers is different for each person, and some people need further treatment.
- **Erections:** recovery of erections, if it happens, can take many months or longer and varies a great deal. Medicines and other treatments can help, so ask about a rehabilitation plan.
- **Fertility:** after this operation you will no longer ejaculate semen and cannot father children naturally. If this matters to you, ask about sperm banking **before** surgery.
- **PSA follow-up:** you will need regular PSA blood tests. Arrange a local urologist before you leave India, and ask your surgeon when the first test is due.

## Fitness to fly

- **Catheter:** many patients prefer to fly after the catheter is removed. If you must travel with one, carry spare bags and supplies and a letter from your doctor. Empty the bag before boarding, and choose an aisle seat near a toilet.
- **Blood clots:** pelvic surgery and cancer both increase the risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis, DVT). Ask about blood-thinning injections and compression stockings, and walk and stretch during the flight.
- **Gas pain:** the gas used in keyhole surgery is absorbed over a few days, and your doctor will check you are comfortable.
- **Supplies and paperwork:** carry pads, medicines and your discharge summary in your hand luggage. Ask for wheelchair assistance if needed. If your doctor has concerns, the airline may require a medical clearance form, often called a MEDIF.

Your treating doctor confirms when you are fit to fly, usually at a review before discharge or shortly after.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- What is the stage and risk group of my cancer, and what are all my treatment options?
- Why do you recommend surgery rather than radiotherapy or active surveillance?
- Will you try to spare the nerves involved in erections, and what affects whether that is possible?
- Will you remove lymph nodes?
- How often do you perform this operation?
- What are the risks, such as bleeding, infection, leakage, erection problems, or narrowing where the bladder is rejoined?
- How long will I have the catheter, and can I fly with it?
- What help is available for bladder control and erections afterwards?
- When will I get the final pathology results, and what happens if they show cancer at the edges of the removed tissue or in the lymph nodes?
- How will PSA follow-up be arranged at home?
- Should I consider sperm banking before surgery?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** Get urgent help if:
>
> - the catheter stops draining and your lower abdomen becomes painful or swollen
> - the catheter falls out (contact the hospital immediately and do not try to put it back)
> - you have a fever or chills, or feel very unwell
> - there is heavy bleeding or large clots in your urine, or bleeding from a wound
> - you have severe or worsening abdominal pain or swelling, or keep vomiting
> - you have chest pain, shortness of breath, or pain and swelling in one leg
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number.
