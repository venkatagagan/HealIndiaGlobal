# Sleeve gastrectomy (weight-loss surgery)

This page explains laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, a type of weight-loss (bariatric) surgery. It is general information to help you prepare questions for your surgical team. It does not tell you whether the operation is right for you.

## What it is

A sleeve gastrectomy permanently removes a large part of the stomach, leaving a narrow tube, or "sleeve". It is usually done by keyhole (laparoscopic) surgery under general anaesthesia. The surgeon works through several small cuts using a camera and fine instruments.

After surgery your stomach holds much less food, so you feel full sooner. The operation also changes some of the hormones involved in hunger and blood sugar. The removed part of the stomach cannot be put back, so the change is permanent.

Surgery is only one part of a long-term programme. Your health afterwards also depends on lasting changes to eating, activity and follow-up care.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Doctors may suggest weight-loss surgery when someone is living with severe obesity, especially if they also have weight-related conditions such as type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure or sleep apnoea, and other approaches have not been enough. The surgical team decides eligibility after assessing your weight history, your general health, your medicines and how ready you are for lifelong changes. The criteria differ between guidelines and countries.

Only your doctors can decide whether this operation is right for you. Ask about the alternatives, such as medically supervised weight-management programmes, weight-loss medicines and other operations like gastric bypass, and why a sleeve is being suggested for you. If you have frequent heartburn or acid reflux, mention it early, because it can affect which operation is advised.

## Records to share for a quote

- Your height, current weight and a short weight history, including what you have tried before
- A list of your health conditions, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, sleep apnoea, or heart or lung problems
- Recent blood test results, and any endoscopy, ultrasound or sleep-study reports
- Details of previous operations, especially on the abdomen, with operation notes if you have them
- Your current medicines (including insulin, other injections and weight-loss medicines) and any allergies
- Discharge summaries from recent hospital stays, and your smoking and alcohol history

## Tests usually done before treatment

- Blood tests, such as a full blood count, kidney and liver function, blood sugar and HbA1c, thyroid function, clotting, blood group, and often vitamin and iron levels
- An upper endoscopy (a camera test of the food pipe and stomach) in many centres
- An abdominal ultrasound, for example to look for gallstones or a fatty liver
- An ECG and a chest X-ray, and sometimes an echocardiogram or lung function tests
- Screening for sleep apnoea if it is suspected
- Assessments by a dietitian and an anaesthetist, and often a psychological or behavioural assessment

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need; some may be repeated in India. Some teams ask you to follow a special low-calorie diet for a short time before surgery to make the operation safer. Ask whether this should start at home before you travel.

## Typical time in hospital and in India

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Assessment before surgery | Often 1–2 days | Tests, consultations and an anaesthesia check after you arrive |
| Hospital stay | About 2–3 days | You walk soon after surgery and start a liquid diet |
| Total time in India | About 1–2 weeks | Assessment, surgery, early recovery and a review before flying |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

- **Moving early:** you will be encouraged to get up and walk soon after surgery. This helps lower the risk of blood clots and chest infections.
- **Staged diet:** you usually start with clear liquids, then move to full liquids, purées and soft foods over several weeks before normal textures. Follow the written plan from your dietitian.
- **Fluids:** take small sips regularly through the day. The smaller stomach makes it harder to drink large amounts at once.
- **Wounds:** keep the small cuts clean and dry, as instructed. Ask whether your stitches dissolve or need removing.
- **Wind pain:** pain at the tip of the shoulder is common after keyhole surgery. It comes from the gas used to inflate the abdomen and usually settles as the gas is absorbed over a few days.
- **Medicines:** take pain relief, acid-reducing medicine and any blood-thinning injections exactly as prescribed.

### After you return home

- Keep moving through the diet stages as your team advises. Eat slowly, take small portions and stop when you feel full.
- You will usually need vitamin and mineral supplements for life, with regular blood tests for levels such as vitamin B12, iron, calcium and vitamin D. Your team will tell you which supplements to take.
- **Before you travel**, arrange a local doctor and, ideally, a dietitian who can see you when you get home.
- Increase activity gradually. Avoid heavy lifting for as long as your surgeon advises.
- If you could become pregnant, ask how long to wait before trying, because rapid weight loss affects nutrition.
- Eating habits and body image can change a lot. Support groups and counselling can help.

## Fitness to fly

- **Blood clots:** both recent surgery and a higher body weight increase the risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis, DVT) on long flights. Ask whether you need blood-thinning injections or compression stockings, and how often to walk and stretch on board.
- **Fluids on board:** sip water regularly, because you cannot drink large amounts at once. Ask for a letter if you need to carry liquid supplements through airport security.
- **Comfort:** an aisle seat makes walking easier. Ask the airline in advance about seating and a seat-belt extender if you think you may need one.
- **Paperwork:** some airlines ask for a medical clearance form, often called a MEDIF. Ask for wheelchair or airport assistance if walking long distances is difficult.
- **Medicines:** carry your medicines, supplements and discharge summary in your hand luggage.

Your treating doctor confirms when you are fit to fly, usually at a review before discharge or shortly after.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Why are you suggesting a sleeve rather than another operation or a non-surgical option?
- What do my test results mean for my risks, given my other health conditions?
- Do I need a special diet before surgery, and when should it start?
- How often does your team perform this operation?
- What are the possible complications, such as a leak, bleeding or reflux, and how would they be managed?
- Which of my medicines need adjusting before or after surgery? (Do not stop any medicine unless a doctor tells you to.)
- Can I have my diet plan for the first weeks in writing?
- Which vitamins and supplements will I need, and for how long?
- Which blood tests will I need at home, and how often?
- When will I be fit to fly, and what should I do to lower my clot risk?
- How can I contact your team after I go home?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** Get urgent medical help if, after surgery, you have:
>
> - fever, a fast heartbeat, or you feel very unwell
> - severe or worsening pain in the abdomen, chest or shoulder
> - repeated vomiting, or you cannot keep fluids down
> - vomit containing blood, or black, tarry stools
> - shortness of breath or chest pain
> - pain, swelling or redness in one leg
> - redness, swelling or discharge from a wound
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number.
