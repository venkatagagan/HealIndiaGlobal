# Spinal fusion surgery

This page gives a general explanation of spinal fusion surgery. It can help you plan your trip and prepare questions for your spine surgeon. Your own doctors will advise on your situation.

## What it is

Spinal fusion joins two or more bones of the spine (vertebrae) so that they heal into one solid piece of bone. This stops movement between them. That can help when the movement causes pain, instability or a change in the shape of the spine.

The surgeon places bone graft between or around the vertebrae. The graft may come from your own body or from a donor, or it may be a man-made substitute. Metal screws, rods or spacers (cages) hold the bones steady while they fuse. Fusion is often combined with decompression. This means removing bone or disc material that presses on nerves.

The surgeon can reach the spine from the back, the front or the side. Some operations use minimally invasive techniques with smaller cuts. Surgery is done under general anaesthesia. The bones fuse gradually over the months after the operation.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Doctors may suggest spinal fusion for problems such as:

- a vertebra slipping forward (spondylolisthesis)
- an unstable spine
- some fractures
- curvature of the spine (scoliosis or kyphosis)
- pressure on a nerve when the spine is also unstable

Fusion is usually considered after other treatments have not helped enough.

Back pain has many causes, and fusion is not suitable for everyone with back pain. Only your doctors can decide whether it is right for you. Ask about the alternatives:

- physiotherapy
- pain management
- injections
- decompression without fusion
- for some people, disc replacement

A second opinion before spine surgery is reasonable.

## Records to share for a quote

Upload:

- Your most recent MRI scan, as images (DICOM files) and report
- CT scans and X-rays, including standing X-rays or bending views if you have them
- Nerve test results (nerve conduction studies or EMG), if you have had them
- Details of any previous spine surgery or implants
- What treatments you have tried, such as physiotherapy, injections or pain medicines
- A bone density (DEXA) scan, if you have had one
- A full list of your medicines and doses, including pain medicines, blood thinners and steroids
- Any allergies
- Whether you smoke or use nicotine, because this affects bone healing

## Tests usually done before treatment

Typical tests include:

- Blood tests: blood count, kidney and liver function, blood sugar, clotting, blood group, and infection screening
- A urine test, an ECG and a chest X-ray
- An updated MRI, CT scan or standing X-rays
- A bone density scan, for some patients
- Nerve tests, for some patients
- An assessment by the anaesthetist

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need. Some may be repeated in India.

## Typical time in hospital and in India

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital stay | About 3–6 days | Longer if several levels are fused or the operation is more complex |
| Total time in India | About 2–4 weeks | Covers tests, surgery, early recovery and a wound check before you fly |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

You will usually be helped to get up and walk soon after surgery, often the next day. Physiotherapists will teach you how to get in and out of bed safely, often by rolling onto your side first. They will also show you how to move without bending or twisting your spine.

You may be given a brace. Pain will be controlled with medicines. The team will check:

- your wound
- the strength and feeling in your legs
- how well your bladder is working

### After you return home

- Follow your surgeon's limits on bending, lifting and twisting. These usually last for weeks to months.
- Walk a little more each day. Sitting for long periods may be uncomfortable at first, so change position often.
- Before you travel, arrange a physiotherapist near your home. Start physiotherapy when your surgeon advises.
- Avoid smoking and nicotine. They can slow down or stop the bones from fusing.
- Follow your team's advice on driving and returning to work.
- Your doctors will use follow-up X-rays or CT scans to check how the fusion is progressing.
- Keep your implant details for future scans and procedures.

## Fitness to fly

Your treating doctor will confirm when you are fit to fly. This is usually at a review before you leave hospital or shortly after. Things to discuss:

- **Sitting for a long time:** choose an aisle seat so that you can get up and walk. Take a small cushion to support your lower back. Consider breaking up a long journey if you can.
- **Blood clots (DVT):** ask whether you need compression stockings and which exercises to do. Drink water and move regularly.
- **Lifting:** do not lift luggage. Ask for help at check-in and on board.
- **Assistance:** ask for a wheelchair or airport assistance.
- **Airport security:** metal implants can set off detectors. Carry your implant card or a letter.
- **Pain medicines:** some strong pain medicines are controlled substances in some countries. Carry your prescription and a doctor's letter. Check the rules of your home country and of any country you pass through.
- **Airline medical clearance:** after recent surgery, some airlines ask for a medical form, often called a MEDIF. Check with your airline early.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Why do you recommend fusion, and what are the alternatives for me?
- How many levels will be fused, and which approach will you use?
- Which implants and bone graft will you use?
- What are the main risks, including nerve injury and the bones not fusing?
- Will I need a brace?
- What should I avoid, and for how long?
- When can I safely fly home, and how should I manage a long flight?
- When can I return to driving and work?
- What follow-up scans will I need, and when?
- Which pain medicines will I go home with, and can I take them through airports?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** Get help immediately if you have any of these:
>
> - new or worsening weakness or numbness in your legs
> - loss of bladder or bowel control
> - numbness around your genitals or buttocks
> - a fever or chills
> - a wound that is red, hot or leaking, including clear fluid
> - a severe headache that gets worse when you sit or stand
> - a painful, swollen calf
> - chest pain or sudden breathlessness
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number.
