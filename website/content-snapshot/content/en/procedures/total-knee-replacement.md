# Total knee replacement

This page gives a general explanation of total knee replacement. It can help you plan your trip and prepare questions for your orthopaedic surgeon. Your own doctors will advise on your situation.

## What it is

Total knee replacement is also called total knee arthroplasty. The surgeon removes the damaged surfaces of the knee joint and replaces them with artificial parts (an implant) made of metal and medical-grade plastic. The ends of the thigh bone and shin bone are reshaped and capped. The back of the kneecap may also be resurfaced.

The operation is usually done under spinal anaesthesia, sometimes with a nerve block for pain relief. It can also be done under general anaesthesia. Some hospitals use computer navigation or a robotic arm to help the surgeon. Ask your surgeon what they use and why.

If only one part of your knee is damaged, a partial knee replacement may be an option. If both knees need replacing, they may be done in the same operation or in two stages.

## Why doctors may recommend it

Doctors may suggest knee replacement when the knee is very painful and stiff and this limits walking, daily activities or sleep. The cause is usually arthritis, most often osteoarthritis or rheumatoid arthritis, or an old injury. Surgery is usually considered when non-surgical treatments have not helped enough. These include:

- exercise and physiotherapy
- weight management
- pain relief
- walking aids
- injections

Only your doctors can decide whether surgery is right for you. Ask about the alternatives, including continuing non-surgical care and partial knee replacement.

## Records to share for a quote

Upload:

- Recent knee X-rays, as images and reports. Standing views are best if you have them.
- An MRI or CT scan, if you have had one
- Details of any previous knee surgery or implants
- Recent blood tests
- Details of other conditions, such as diabetes or heart disease
- A full list of your medicines and doses, especially blood thinners, steroids and medicines that affect your immune system
- Any allergies, including to metals or antibiotics
- Your height and weight, and a short description of your pain and how far you can walk

## Tests usually done before treatment

Typical tests include:

- Blood tests: blood count, kidney and liver function, blood sugar and HbA1c, clotting, blood group, and infection screening
- A urine test
- An ECG and a chest X-ray, and for some patients an echocardiogram
- Updated knee X-rays, and sometimes a CT scan for robotic or custom planning
- Checks for any infection that should be treated before surgery, for example of the skin, urine or teeth
- An assessment by the anaesthetist

Your hospital will tell you which tests you need. Some may be repeated in India.

## Typical time in hospital and in India

| Stage | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital stay | About 3–5 days | Often longer if both knees are replaced together |
| Total time in India | About 2–3 weeks | Longer if both knees are replaced; covers physiotherapy and wound checks before you fly |

These are typical ranges only. Your time varies by patient; your doctor will advise.

## Recovery

### In India

Physiotherapy usually starts on the day of surgery or the next day. You will learn to:

- walk with a frame or crutches
- climb stairs safely
- do exercises to bend and straighten your knee

Pain, swelling and bruising are expected. The team will control your pain so that you can exercise.

You may be given injections or tablets, compression stockings and leg exercises. These lower the risk of blood clots. Before you fly, the team will check your wound and remove stitches or clips if needed.

### After you return home

- Before you travel, arrange a physiotherapist near your home. Take the surgeon's exercise programme with you.
- Keep up your exercises every day. Strength and movement keep improving for months, and some swelling can last a long time.
- Use your walking aid until your physiotherapist or surgeon says you can stop.
- Follow your team's advice on kneeling, driving and returning to work.
- Keep the details of your implant, including the manufacturer and model.
- Tell doctors and dentists you have a joint replacement. Ask your surgeon whether you will need antibiotics before future procedures.
- Arrange a follow-up review with an orthopaedic surgeon, either at home or by video consultation. Have X-rays done if the surgeon asks for them.

## Fitness to fly

Your treating doctor will confirm when you are fit to fly. This is usually at a review before you leave hospital or shortly after. Things to discuss:

- **Blood clots (DVT):** the risk is higher after leg surgery, and long flights add to it. Ask about blood thinners, compression stockings and exercises. Choose an aisle seat, and get up and walk when it is safe to do so.
- **Comfort:** a seat with extra legroom helps you keep the knee moving and reduces swelling.
- **Assistance:** ask for a wheelchair and help at airports. Take your walking aid with you.
- **Airport security:** metal implants can set off security detectors. Carry your implant card or a letter from your surgeon.
- **Airline medical clearance:** after recent surgery, some airlines ask for a medical form, often called a MEDIF. Check with your airline early.
- **Medicines:** keep them in your hand luggage, with your prescription.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Do I need a total knee replacement, or could a partial replacement or non-surgical care work for me?
- Which implant will you use, and why?
- Do you use navigation or robotic assistance? What difference does it make for me?
- If both knees are affected, should they be done together or separately?
- What are the main risks, such as infection, blood clots or stiffness?
- How will my pain be controlled?
- How will blood clots be prevented, both during my stay and on the flight home?
- How long will I need walking aids?
- When can I safely fly home?
- What should my physiotherapist at home know?
- Will I get an implant card?

## When to get urgent help

> **Emergency:** Get help immediately if you have any of these:
>
> - chest pain or sudden breathlessness, which can be a sign of a blood clot in the lungs
> - a painful, swollen calf or thigh
> - a fever or chills
> - a wound that is red, hot, leaking or opening
> - a sudden increase in pain, or being unable to put weight on the leg
> - heavy bleeding
> - a foot that becomes cold, pale or numb
>
> In India, call 112 or the hospital. At home, call your local emergency number.
